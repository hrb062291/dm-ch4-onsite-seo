Digital Marketing · Chapter 4

On-Site SEO

On-site SEO is everything you control on your own website. This chapter shows how a search engine reads a page, how it decides a page is relevant, and what you can change so the right pages rank for the right words. Earning links from other sites (off-site SEO) is Chapter 5.

Learning objectives

By the end of this chapter you should be able to:

1 · Choose keywords

Identify the keywords most worth targeting for a website.

2 · Improve relevance

Suggest specific changes that make a page more relevant for its target keyword.

3 · Improve quality signals

Suggest site changes that make search engines trust the site more.

4 · Find technical problems

Spot technical issues that could be holding back a site's organic rankings.

Chapter review · Part 1

How a search engine works

The original Google research paper by Sergey Brin and Larry Page described the parts of a search engine. The technology has changed a lot since then, but the basic flow still holds, and it explains why on-site SEO works the way it does.

STEP 1
CrawlA URL server keeps a list of pages to visit. Crawlers follow links to find new and updated pages.
STEP 2
StoreEach page's HTML is saved in a repository, a stored copy of the web.
STEP 3
IndexEach page is turned into a "hit list": which words appear, where, and how prominently.
STEP 4
SortHit lists are re-sorted by word, so Google can find every page that uses a word.
STEP 5
RankFor a search, Google scores each matching page for relevance and authority, then combines them.

Crawling has limits

Crawlers are fast, but they can't visit every page constantly. Pages that change often get crawled more often. If nothing links to a page, the crawler may never find it.

What's a "hit"?

Here, a hit is one occurrence of a word on a page, not a page view. Each hit records whether the word is capitalized, its font size and its position, for example Cap: 0, font: 3, position: 173.

Google doesn't store your page the way a visitor sees it. It stores which words appear, where they appear, and how prominent they are. That's why the HTML tags later in this chapter matter so much.

Try it: from pages to an inverted index

A list sorted by page can't answer "which pages contain this word?" So the search engine flips it: for every word, a list of pages. Type a word (or pick one) to look it up the way the index does.

How links are used

Crawling

Links are how crawlers find pages.

Listing pages

Every link found adds a page (docID) to the list.

Relevance

The clickable words of a link (anchor text) describe the page it points to.

Authority

Links from other sites act like votes. This is the idea behind PageRank.

Ranking = relevance × authority. This chapter is about relevance and site quality. Chapter 5 covers authority (links).
What changed since the textbook
  • Google removed the public "Cached" link from search results in 2024. To see how Google sees one of your pages, use the URL Inspection tool in Google Search Console.
  • The textbook says PageRank is "no longer used." What Google retired in 2016 was the public PageRank score in its toolbar. Google has said PageRank is still one of many signals, and link-based signals still matter.

Calculating relevance

When someone searches "metamorphosis," Google finds every page containing that word and gives each one a relevance score. In the simplified model from the textbook, each hit is weighted by where the word appears.

Hit typeWeight
URL100
Title tag100
Anchor text90
Plain text, large font60
Plain text, medium font30
Plain text, small font10

The lesson of the weights

One appearance in the URL or title tag is worth ten appearances in small body text. Where a word appears matters more than how many times it appears.

Count-weights: why keyword stuffing stopped working

Webmasters used to repeat a keyword dozens of times, sometimes in white text so visitors couldn't see it. Count-weights stop this: the first two hits of each type count fully, then each extra hit counts less, and from the ninth hit on, extra hits count nothing.

Hit number123456789+
Weight11.9.7.45.2.05.010
With count-weights, the textbook scores become 820 vs. 751. A 1,710-point gap shrinks to 69. Beyond about five mentions, repeating a keyword adds almost nothing, and hidden text violates Google's spam policies.

Multi-word searches

A search for butterfly metamorphosis is scored three ways:

  • "butterfly"
  • "metamorphosis"
  • the phrase "butterfly metamorphosis"

Why this matters

Far fewer pages match the whole phrase, so there's less competition. It's much easier to rank for multi-word ("long-tail") searches, which is where new and small sites can actually win.

Chapter review · Part 3

Keyword usage and choosing keywords

Look at Wikipedia's "Surfing" article. The word appears in the URL, the title, the main heading, bold text in the first sentence, image captions and the links pointing to the page. Wikipedia never forces it in. It's everywhere because the page is really about surfing.

The six places a keyword should appear

1

URL

The page address, e.g. /kids/girls-clothing/activewear.

2

Title tag

The <title> in the HTML. It's the blue headline in search results.

3

Header tags

Headings like <h1> and <h2>. Each page should have one clear H1.

4

Main content

The body text. Natural wording beats forced repetition.

5

Alt text

The text description of an image. It helps screen readers and image search.

6

Anchor text

The clickable words of a link pointing to the page, including menu links.

Four criteria for choosing keywords

In class you'll practice with the Alta Electronics case. Use these four tests on every candidate keyword:

Relevance

Does the page actually satisfy the searcher? Traffic for the wrong word just produces visitors who leave.

Traffic

How many people search for it each month?

Competition

How many strong sites already target it?

Current ranking

Moving from #11 to #5 is much cheaper than moving from #80 to #5.

Relevance comes first. A high-volume keyword you can't satisfy is worth less than a modest one you can.
Chapter review · Part 4

Keyword mapping

A keyword map assigns a target keyword to each page of a site. It follows the site's structure: broad terms at the top, specific terms further down.

  1. One term per page. If two pages target the same term, they compete with each other. This is called keyword cannibalization.
  2. Relevant, high volume, low competition. The best terms meet all three tests.
  3. "Fat head" terms go higher up. Broad, high-volume terms belong on the homepage and category pages. Specific "long-tail" terms belong on product pages.

Chapter review · Part 5

Quality signals

Relevance tells Google a page is about the search. Quality signals tell Google whether the page is worth showing.

The 'Back' button

If searchers click your result and quickly return to the results page, that suggests your page missed. Testimony in the U.S. antitrust trial confirmed that Google uses click data in ranking.

Meta description

The summary under your result. It doesn't change your rank directly, but it changes how many people click.

Speed

Slow pages lose visitors. Google measures page experience with Core Web Vitals: loading (LCP), responsiveness (INP) and visual stability (CLS).

Original content

Copying text from other sites, including manufacturer descriptions, gives Google no reason to prefer your page.

Fresh content

Regular updates give crawlers a reason to return and show the site is active.

Quantity and quality

Enough content to answer the question fully, written for people rather than for search engines.

Most quality signals come down to one question: did the searcher's search end on your page?
Chapter review · Part 6

Technical problems

Technical problems don't make you rank. They stop you from ranking. Check for these first, because no amount of good content fixes a page Google can't find or trust.

ProblemWhat it meansHow to check or fix
Orphan pagesNo other page links to them, so crawlers can't find them.Link every important page from a menu, category page or related page.
Past abusesA domain that was used for spam may carry a penalty.Check Manual Actions in Google Search Console. Before buying a used domain, look up its history on archive.org.
XML sitemapA file listing the pages you want indexed.Create one (most site builders do it automatically) and submit it in Search Console.
Mobile usabilityGoogle uses the mobile version of a site for indexing and ranking.Test with Lighthouse or PageSpeed Insights. Make sure the mobile site has the same content as desktop.
SecurityHTTPS is a small ranking signal, and a "hacked site" warning destroys clicks.Use HTTPS everywhere and keep plugins and software updated.
What changed since the textbook Google Search Console retired its Mobile Usability report in December 2023; use Lighthouse or PageSpeed Insights instead. Google finished moving to mobile-first indexing in 2024, so the mobile version of your site is now the version that counts.
Chapter review · Part 7

Local search results

For searches like "coffee near me," Google shows a map with a handful of businesses before the regular results. For a local business, that map can matter more than any organic ranking.

Google Business Profile

The free listing that powers the map: hours, photos, category, website and posts. Claim it and keep it complete.

NAP consistency

Name, Address and Phone should be identical everywhere: your site, Yelp, directories and social profiles.

Customer reviews

The number of reviews, the ratings and your responses all affect local rankings and whether people choose you.

What changed since the textbook Google My Business was renamed Google Business Profile in 2021, and profiles are now managed directly from Google Search and Maps.
Chapter review · Part 8

How Google evolved

Each major update closed a shortcut that people had used to rank without deserving it.

1998PageRank: ranks pages by the links pointing to them.
2011Panda: demotes thin, low-quality and copied content.
2012Penguin: demotes sites that buy or fake links.
2012Knowledge Graph: understands people, places and things, not just strings of words.
2013Hummingbird: understands what a whole query means.
2014Pigeon: improves local results using traditional ranking signals.
2015RankBrain: machine learning for queries Google hasn't seen before.
2016–2024Mobile-first indexing: the mobile version of a site becomes the one Google indexes.
2019BERT: understands words in context (not in the textbook).
2022–2024Helpful content and AI Overviews: rewards content written for people; AI summaries now appear above many results (not in the textbook).
Every update punished a shortcut. The strategy that survives all of them is to be the best answer to the search.
Lecture

The 5 components of SEO

The first three describe the whole website. The last two describe individual pages.

Site

1 · Size

More pages means more chances to rank. getrest.co is mostly a single scrolling page, so it has very few pages and places to put keywords. One page can only target one main keyword.

Site

2 · Time

Older sites tend to rank better, because they've had time to build links and content. If you're building a new site, launch a basic version as soon as possible.

Site

3 · Popularity

Links from other websites. SEO professionals rank this as the most important factor. Link building is Chapter 5.

Page

4 · Linking

Internal links help crawlers find pages and tell Google which pages are most important. Pages with no internal links are orphans.

Page

5 · Text

The words on a page are how Google judges its relevance to a search.

Learning from experts

Nordstrom: the six places, in practice

Nordstrom is a large retailer with a strong SEO team. The textbook checks the six keyword places on a category page (Girls' Activewear) and then on a product page (a kids' fleece pullover).

PlaceCategory page: Girls' ActivewearProduct page: Kids' Fleece Pullover
URL/kids/girls-clothing/activewear. The folders follow the site's structure./s/kids-fleece-pullover-little-kid-big-kid/7456464. Words for people and search engines; the number is for Nordstrom's database.
Title tag"Girls' Activewear Clothing | Nordstrom." Keyword first, brand last."Zella Girl Kids' Fleece Pullover." Brand plus product, because people search for brands.
Header (H1)"Girls' Activewear Clothing," an exact match for the title."Kids' Fleece Pullover," the title minus the brand.
Main contentAlmost no text, just product names and images. Natural, not forced.The same description appears twice and is probably the manufacturer's text, which other stores use too.
Alt text"Kids' Fleece Pullover (Little Kid & Big Kid)." Describes the product, not the category."Zella Girl Kids' Fleece Pullover, Alternate, color, BLUE BROOK." Generated from product data.
Anchor text"Activewear" in the Kids menu links here. Menu labels are a keyword decision.The category page links here with "Kids' Fleece Pullover (Little Kid & Big Kid)."
When the URL, title, heading and links all say the same thing, Google has no doubt what the page is about. That consistency is the main lesson from Nordstrom.

See it yourself

Title tag

Right-click an empty part of a page and choose View Page Source. Search (Ctrl/Cmd+F) for <title.

Header tags

In the same page source, search for <h1.

Alt text

Right-click an image and choose Inspect. Look for alt="…" in the highlighted code.

After the click

Bounce rate

A searcher for "womens shoes" landed on a page whose first screen showed a lipstick ad and an editorial feature, with the shoes below. Relevant page, questionable first impression. If they hit Back, Google notices.

Load time

Retail pages are heavy with images and data. Run PageSpeed Insights on any big retailer and compare mobile with desktop.

Fresh content

New products, sales and promotions, articles, reviews and Q&A. Reviews and Q&A are free, original text written by customers, which fixes the duplicate-description problem.

Heads up The textbook screenshots are from a few years ago. Nordstrom's live site will look different, but the same six checks still apply.

Winning the click

Ranking gets your page seen. Three things decide whether people click it: the title tag, the meta description and the URL. Google sometimes rewrites titles and descriptions it thinks don't fit the search, and rich results (images, prices, ratings) come from structured data on the page.

Practice

Exercise: build a keyword map

Your group will be assigned one of these sites:

tcfsales.com zena.net accuratepartitions.com merhow.com

Steps

  1. Determine the navigation menu. Sketch the site's structure as a tree: homepage, then category pages.
  2. Choose keywords for the homepage and category pages. One keyword per page.
  3. Justify each choice in one line using relevance, traffic, competition and current ranking.

Before you turn it in, check for:

  • Cannibalization: are two pages targeting the same term (or near-synonyms like "desk" and "desks")?
  • Fat head placement: are broad terms on the homepage and categories, not on narrow pages?
  • Menu labels: does the menu text (anchor text) match each page's keyword?
  • Search volume: did you check volume with a keyword tool, rather than guess?
